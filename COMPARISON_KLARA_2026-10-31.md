# Comparison with Klara Kuemmerle's c40_metrics

31 October 2026. Compared: the `c40_metrics` folder Klara shared on Dropbox (downloaded as a zip the same evening) against the pipeline we rebuilt in `code/`, the shipped files in `archive/original_2026-10-31/`, and the site as it now runs. Her code, documents, notebook and workbook are copied to `archive/klara_c40_metrics_2026-10-31/`; the figures and the daily panel stay in the zip and on Dropbox.

## What the full version holds

Her folder is a git repository with one commit, "Initial commit" of 31 October 12:36 EDT, and a set of later uncommitted edits (spelling, the renumbering of the site export to step 7 after the paragraph writer became step 6, and a station-check section added to the methods document). The remote on GitHub is not reachable, so the Dropbox copy is the only one we have. It contains seven numbered scripts and a `config.py`, a shell driver and a notebook that runs them, three documents (`methods_and_assumptions.md`, `translation_guide.md`, `city_paragraphs_DRAFT.md`), the workbook `C40_ENSO_metrics.xlsx`, 91 of the 96 city figures, the site, the processed tables, the daily panel and the RONI file. The raw ERA5 city files that her step 1 reads (`c40_daily/*.parquet`) are not in the share, so the daily panel is the earliest input either version has.

Every file we had received before is byte-identical to hers: the four processed tables, the daily panel, the RONI file, `site/index.html` and all 98 data files under `site/data/`. The review and the rebuild were therefore done against her final outputs. The site's paragraphs are her unedited draft; `meta.json` records `city_paragraphs_DRAFT.md` as the source, and no hand-edited `city_paragraphs.md` exists. The draft carries thirteen reviewer notes in eleven cities that the export leaves out. The notebook shows the order of the last runs: the quantile step was last started with 30 bootstrap draws and interrupted after 48 of 576 sets, so the shipped quantile table comes from an earlier full run with 100 draws, which the `fits` column confirms. The figures and the workbook came from that run too. Five figures (Heidelberg, London, Montréal, Warsaw, Zhenjiang) did not make it into the share although the notebook reports 96 saved.

## Step by step against the rebuild

Her step 1 builds the seasonal and daily panels from the ERA5 city files in one pass; ours builds the seasonal panel from the daily panel. The aggregation rules are the same (97% of days, wet day at 1 mm, December assigned to the following year) and our panel matches hers to 1e-13.

Her linear model is the one we reconstructed, down to the trend-only fit for the partial R², the 2027 level and its variance and covariance with the El Niño slope, and one Benjamini–Hochberg family of 768 tests. Our table matches hers to 1e-12.

The quantile step is where the two differ. She fits each percentile by iteratively reweighted least squares (`fast_quantreg`, 300 iterations at a tolerance of 1e-6 for the point estimate, 150 at 1e-5 for each bootstrap draw, warm-started from the point estimate) and seeds the year-block bootstrap from a hash of the city, season and variable. We fit with statsmodels' QuantReg, which stops at the linear-programming optimum, and draw with a different seed. This accounts for everything we reported in the rebuild: her point estimates sit a few thousandths of a standard error from the optimum, 12.6% of her fits stop at the iteration cap against 1.1% of ours, and the bootstrap spreads differ within Monte Carlo noise, which flips the FDR flag on 46 of 1,701 tests and the tail verdict on 27 of 567. Both versions skip the nine dry cells with fewer than 300 wet days. Our table is therefore not a reproduction of hers but a re-estimate of the same model, and we would get her numbers back only by porting her solver and seed.

Her step 5, the facts file, is what our step 4 computes in legacy mode, and the outputs agree exactly. Reading her code showed one threshold we had guessed loosely: the years-of-warming equivalent is reported when the temperature trend is positive with p below 0.05, not 0.10. No cell falls between the two bars, so no output changed, but we have aligned the code and re-validated legacy mode at zero differences.

Her step 3 builds a workbook we have no equivalent for: eight tabs, with the fitted statistics written as values and every derived column (confidence and size labels, this season's effect, the 2027 center and spread, the tercile probabilities, the return period, the words) as a live formula reading a Settings tab that holds the RONI assumption and its spread. We checked the 768 Linear rows against `linear_results.csv` and found no mismatch, and the formulas are the facts code transcribed. The file has never been calculated, so a reader opens it in Excel and the formulas fill in. Her step 4 draws one figure per city, four rows by two seasons: the RONI bars, then for temperature, wet-day rainfall and humidity the observed yearly percentiles as dots, the fitted quantile lines wiggling with RONI, and a diamond at the 2027 fit. Our pipeline has neither product.

Her step 7, the site export, is the script we reconstructed, and our export reproduces her data files exactly. The site page is identical; the Jupyter checkpoint beside it shows her last edits, which renamed the page from "El Niño Cities Outlook" to "C40 El Niño Outlook" and added the station-check paragraph to the Methods page.

## Her paragraph writer against ours

Her step 6 is the paragraph writer we reconstructed as our step 5, and on the current facts the two write the same 192 paragraphs. We had both scripts read side by side for their rules rather than their output, because a rule recovered from the cases that occurred can be narrower or wider than the rule that produced them, and the reading found both kinds.

Our legacy rules hard-wire today's pattern of confidence labels: a Strong label always gets the strong wording and a Clear label the modest wording, with the one Clear case that survives the FDR correction upgraded by name. Her code derives an effective label, dropping Strong or Clear one step when the effect fails the correction, and lets every clause follow it. A Strong effect that failed FDR, or a Clear temperature effect that survived it, would be worded differently by the two. Her thresholds are guarded where ours are not: the temperature trend sentence needs the slope to round to at least 0.05 °C and writes "cooled" for a cooling city, the fraction clause prints only from 10%, the return-period sentence follows the effective label, the humidity trend needs a tenth of a point per decade, and the warm-air note appears only when temperature is itself rising. Her extremes sentences require that the fitted quantile lines do not cross and that one tail's own effect is Strong or Clear; ours test the tail-difference p alone. Her rainy-days clause requires the wet-day effect to point the same way as the rainfall effect, and her heavy-rain clause asks the same of the 95th-percentile slope; ours follow the rainfall direction. Her outlook adds the extrapolation sentence when the RONI assumption exceeds the fitted record and drops the closing sentence once the RONI is observed; ours never writes the first and always writes the second. She rounds half to even and we round half up, which diverges only on exact ties. The rule we had flagged as resting on a single case, leaving out the rainy-days clause where more than 90% of days are wet, is a general rule in her code with a reviewer note behind it. Our overrides file reproduces her station-check and confirmed-trend tables, with three differences: her Vancouver entry also adds the "partly because of an uncertain long-term drying trend" clause to the outlook, her Santiago autumn "no clear trend" is a station-check entry where ours is a sign test, and her dry-season check runs before the station check where ours runs after.

Her script has faults of its own that the current data never trip: an `if True:` that makes the alternative rainfall join unreachable, an operator-precedence slip that would silently drop every outlook clause if a third one ever appeared, "roughly 1 points", a ratio of exactly 1.25 that prints "about 1 times", a tail-trend clause written only for warming trends, a tail-difference p of exactly zero treated as missing, and a total warming computed from the unrounded slope while the per-decade rate is rounded.

Our new-rules mode shares its sentence builders with the legacy mode, so the gaps would have carried over to the December update, when the observed RONI moves effects across these bars. We therefore ported her general rules into our generator the same evening, keeping our command line, the two rule sets and the new set's features, and moved her station-check and confirmed-trend tables into `code/paragraph_overrides.json` in her form. The legacy output still matches the shipped text in all 192 paragraphs; the new-rules output changed in five paragraphs besides the closing sentence, which now names the upper range of the RONI estimate while it lies beyond the record. Her faults were left out rather than copied, and `docs/PARAGRAPH_GENERATOR_NOTES.md` lists what was ported and what was not.

## Where her documents and her code disagree

The methods document says the bootstrap uses 200 draws; the shipped table used 100 and the config now says 30, labelled as a quick test. It says FDR runs across 1,728 quantile tests; the table has 1,701 because of the nine skipped cells. Its station-check section cites Sections 36 and 39 of "the main notebook", which is not in the folder, so the sentence on the Methods page that weather stations agree with ERA5 on the direction of El Niño effects still rests on evidence we have not seen. We had removed that sentence from our page for that reason; her document gives the trend checks in a table with station rates, which is more than we had, and we leave the sentence out until the notebook turns up.

The RONI assumption is hand-set at +2.2 ± 0.25 in `config.py`, with the note that CPC's 8 October discussion gives an 83% chance of RONI reaching +2.5. We checked the discussion today: the 83% is for October–December, and the chance for September–November, the season the model uses, is 54%. Her assumption and our regression estimate of +2.16 ± 0.24 both sit below CPC's own view of the season; the question resolves itself in early December.

The translation guide and the generator also part ways. The guide promises sentences the code never writes: the Weak phrase ("weak hints of an El Niño effect, not distinguishable from chance"; the code treats Weak as no effect), the ratio stated "as a percentage once" with "this year it is the expected outcome", the typical-El-Niño size sentence, the "treat it as a tilt, not a forecast" template, the rainfall and humidity tail sentences, the crossing-lines caveat, and 5 mm rounding for dry normals. The code applies rules the guide does not mention: the station-check and confirmed-trend tables, the 90% wet-day rule, the 10% floor on the fraction clause and the three-to-sixty-year window on the warming equivalent. Anyone editing paragraphs by the guide would write text the generator cannot reproduce, so the guide states the intent and the code is the rule.

## What changed in our folder

We aligned the years-of-warming threshold in `code/04_build_facts.py`, added `archive/klara_c40_metrics_2026-10-31/` with a source note, installed matplotlib and openpyxl into the environment, and added a paragraph to the README. We then rewrote `code/05_write_paragraphs.py` around her rules, regenerated `docs/city_paragraphs.md`, `docs/PARAGRAPH_DIFF.md` and the site data, and checked two city pages. The site still runs on the new rules with the +2.16 ± 0.24 estimate. Nothing is committed.

We did not run her scripts. The session's safety check refuses to execute code from an external source, so the regeneration checks above are static or use our own scripts on her files. Running her steps 3 and 5 to 7 on her tables would take a minute and confirm that her final code reproduces the shipped workbook, facts, draft and site data; running her step 2 with 100 draws would show whether her seeds make the quantile table reproducible on this machine.

## What to take from her version

Two of her products are worth carrying into our pipeline: the workbook, which gives C40 staff every number with the labels recomputed from a cell they can edit, and the per-city figures, which show the quantile fits the paragraphs describe. Both can be built from our tables with a port of her steps 3 and 4 and the new rule set, and the workbook's Settings tab is the natural place for the RONI refresh. Her translation guide is the document we lacked when reading the paragraphs; it states the rules the generator follows and should sit beside our generator notes. Her 30 mm dry-season sentence and our 25 mm silence are the one place the two rule sets disagree on wording for the same facts, and that is a choice for the next release, not a correction.
